\section{$\!\!\!\!\!\!\!$. Real data analysis}    % Section 5

In this section, we evaluate the practical performance of the proposed \BernBino\ distribution on two real datasets of binomial counts with a fixed number of trials $m$: the Women's Mobility data from the Bangladesh Fertility Survey of 1989 and the mathematics test data of de Ayala (2009). Both datasets are over--dispersed and exhibit distributional features (e.g., a secondary mode or a wide, non--Beta shape) that the standard binomial mixture models can hardly capture. For each dataset, we fit the five distributions considered in \S4.2, namely the $\Binomial(m,p)$, the $\BetaBino(m,\al,\be)$, the logit--normal binomial (Logit-N), the Kumaraswamy binomial (KM) and the proposed $\BernBino(m,K,\bla)$, by maximum likelihood (the MM algorithm for \BernBino, and numerical integration with `optim' for the parametric mixtures). The degree $K$ of \BernBino\ is selected over $K = 1, \ldots, 30$ by the BIC. In addition to the in--sample criteria (log--likelihood, AIC, BIC, the L1 distance and the MSE between the fitted and empirical pmfs, and the parametric bootstrap $p$--value of the Pearson statistic with $B=999$ replications), we carry out a five--fold cross--validation repeated three times as an out--of--sample check: on each training fold all models are refitted (with $K$ reselected on that fold by the BIC) and the held--out test log--likelihood (CV logLik) and test L1/MSE are computed. This prevents the flexible \BernBino\ model from being favoured merely by overfitting.

\subsection{Women's mobility data}    % Section 5.1

\emph{Data.} The Women's Mobility data (Huq \& Cleland, 1990; Bartholomew \emph{et al.}, 2002) consist of a rural subsample of $n = 8445$ women from the Bangladesh Fertility Survey of 1989. Each woman was asked whether she could, alone, carry out eight activities: going to any part of the village/town/city, going outside the village/town/city, talking to an unknown man, going to a cinema/cultural show, going shopping, going to a cooperative/mothers' club/other club, attending a political meeting and going to a health centre/hospital. Let $X_i \in \{0,1,\ldots,8\}$ be the number of activities that woman $i$ is permitted to perform; treating the eight items as Bernoulli trials, $X_i \sim \Binomial(8, p_i)$ with a woman--specific mobility level $p_i$. The observed score distribution is strongly over--dispersed and non--unimodal: the counts of $X = 0,\ldots,8$ are $829, 1617, 2369, 1533, 1126, 389, 193, 143, 246$, with a mode at $X=2$ and a clear secondary mode at $X=8$ (246 women, $2.9\%$, who are permitted all eight activities). Such a structure cannot be represented by any unimodal prior on $p$.

\emph{Results.} Table \ref{tab5.mob} reports the in--sample comparison. The \BernBino\ model with the BIC--selected degree $K=15$ attains the largest log--likelihood ($-15929.01$, about $435$ units higher than the best parametric model), the smallest AIC and BIC (lower by about $842$ and $743$ respectively), the smallest L1 distance and MSE, and a bootstrap $p$--value of $1.000$, whereas the binomial, beta--binomial and logit--normal models are all rejected at the $5\%$ level. The BIC curve in Figure \ref{fig5.bic} is minimized at $K=15$ (not at the boundary of the grid), and the log--likelihood of \BernBino\ already exceeds all parametric competitors from $K=5$ onwards. Figure \ref{fig5.mob} compares the fitted score distributions and the residuals: the parametric models systematically underestimate the mass at $X=8$, while the \BernBino\ fit is nearly indistinguishable from the observed frequencies. Table \ref{tab5.mob.cv} shows that the out--of--sample result is even clearer: the CV log--likelihood of \BernBino\ ($-47795.48$) is about $1306$ units higher than that of the best parametric model, and its CV L1 and MSE are roughly halved, with $K=15$ selected by the BIC in all $15$ training folds. Hence the advantage of the proposed model is genuine and not an artefact of overfitting.

The estimated Bernstein prior of the mobility level,
\begin{eqnarray*}
   \pi(p) \;\approx\; 0.92\,\mBeta(p|5,11) + 0.03\,\mBeta(p|4,12) + 0.04\,\mBeta(p|16,1),
\end{eqnarray*}
concentrates about $92\%$ of the mass on a restricted subpopulation (mode around $0.27$) and the remaining mass on a fully unrestricted subpopulation with $p$ close to $1$, which directly reflects the secondary mode at $X=8$ and is impossible for the beta, logit--normal and Kumaraswamy priors. In summary, on the Women's Mobility data the \BernBino\ model is best both in--sample and out--of--sample and provides a flexible, interpretable estimate of the prior density of $p$.

\subsection{Mathematics test data}    % Section 5.2

\emph{Data.} The second dataset is the five--item mathematics test data given by de Ayala (2009, p. 14), which is widely used as a benchmark in item response theory. It is provided as a response--pattern frequency table with a total of $n = 19601$ examinees; each examinee answers $m=5$ binary items, so that the score $X_i \in \{0,1,\ldots,5\}$ is the number of correct answers and $X_i \sim \Binomial(5, p_i)$ with an examinee--specific ability $p_i$. The score distribution is wide and nearly symmetric: the counts of $X = 0,\ldots,5$ are $691, 3099, 4269, 4116, 4041, 3385$. Although the beta--binomial fit is acceptable in--sample, its shape constraints (unimodal beta prior) leave systematic discrepancies that a flexible prior can eliminate.

\emph{Results.} Table \ref{tab5.dea} reports the in--sample comparison. The \BernBino\ model with the BIC--selected degree $K=13$ attains the largest log--likelihood ($-33351.85$, about $164$ units higher than the beta--binomial), the smallest AIC and BIC (lower by about $304$ and $210$ respectively), the smallest L1 distance and MSE, and a bootstrap $p$--value of $1.000$. The out--of--sample results in Table \ref{tab5.dea.cv} confirm the finding: the CV log--likelihood of \BernBino\ ($-100139.2$) is about $414$ units higher than the best parametric model, the CV L1 is almost halved, and $K=8$ is selected by the BIC in all $15$ training folds. The estimated Bernstein prior of the ability, whose weights concentrate on the components $k = 6, 12, 7$ (i.e., a mixture of $\mBeta(7,8)$, $\mBeta(13,2)$ and $\mBeta(8,7)$), is a wide multi--component density that the two--parameter priors cannot reproduce. In summary, the proposed model again outperforms all competitors both in--sample and out--of--sample on this large dataset.

In summary, on both real datasets the proposed \BernBino\ distribution is the best model by every criterion considered, both in--sample and out--of--sample, and it provides a flexible and interpretable estimate of the prior density of the success probability. These results confirm that replacing the fixed beta prior with a Bernstein prior does not sacrifice parsimony in practice when the data are sufficiently informative, and that the extra parameters of \BernBino\ are rewarded rather than punished by the BIC and by out--of--sample validation on datasets with genuinely non--unimodal structure.
