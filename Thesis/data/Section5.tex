\section{$\!\!\!\!\!\!\!$. Real data analysis}    % Section 5

\subsection{Women's mobility data}      % Section 5.1

A rural subsample of the Bangladesh Fertility Survey of 1989, conducted by the \textit{National Institute of Population Research and Training} (NIPORT), Dhaka (\cite{HC1990}). The data were subsequently compiled and analysed by \textcite{GMBS2002}. The data set is distributed with the \tsf{ltm} package for latent variable modelling (\cite{Rizopoulos2006}) and is loaded by \tsf{library(ltm)} and \tsf{data(Mobility)}.

This survey measures the social freedom of women in rural Bangladesh outside the household, containing 8445 samples. Each respondent was asked whether she was able to perform, alone, eight activities of daily life. The activities range from local movement to public participation, so their difficulty and their social acceptability differ substantially, and the degree of freedom varies considerably across women. 

The raw data consist of eight binary item indicators. The variable used for model fitting is the total score
obtained by summing these indicators:

The score distribution is strongly over--dispersed, left--skewed and non--unimodal. The mode is attained at $X = 2$ (28.1 \%), while a clearly separated secondary mode appears at $X = 8$, i.e. 246 women (2.9 \%) may perform all eight activities alone. The distribution therefore mixes a large restricted subpopulation with a small fully unrestricted one, a structure that no unimodal prior on $p$ can represent.

The 8445 women are randomly split into a training set of $n_1 = 5912$ and a test set of $n_2 = 2533$. We perform the distributional fitting of binomial, Beta--Bino, LogitN--Bino, KM--Bino together with Bern--Bino distribution on training set and evaluate their fitting performance on test set.

    \caption{Comparison of the fitting performance for various distributions on the test set.}
    \label{zyf.BSB.fig7}
\end{figure}

Table \ref{zyf.BSB.tab5} reports the final test evaluation: the Bern-Bino distribution achieves the largest test log–likelihood ($-$4769.26, about 142 units higher than others) and the smallest test MSE and $L_1$ (about 0.36 and 0.61 times those of the best parametric distributions), while Figure \ref{zyf.BSB.fig7} shows that its fitted score distribution tracks the test frequencies much more closely than other distributions, especially at the secondary mode $X = 8$. Hence the advantage of the proposed model is genuine and holds on data that never participated in fitting.

\vkL
The estimated Bernstein prior of the mobility level, 
\begin{eqnarray*}
    \pi(p) &\ap& 0.01 \times \mBeta(p | 1, 26) + 0.16 \times \mBeta(p | 7, 20) + 0.78 \times \mBeta(p | 8, 19) \\ [2mm]
    && +\; 0.03 \times \mBeta(p | 24, 3)  + 0.02 \times \mBeta(p | 26, 1), 
\end{eqnarray*}
concentrates about 94\% of the mass on a restricted subpopulation (mode around 0.26) and the remaining mass on a fully unrestricted subpopulation with $p$ close to 1, which directly reflects the secondary mode and is impossible for the beta, logit–normal and Kumaraswamy priors. In summary, on the Women’s Mobility data, the Bern-Bino distribution is best on the test set and provides a flexible, interpretable estimate of the prior density of $p$.
